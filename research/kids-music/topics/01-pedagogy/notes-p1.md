# 01 Pedagogy — P1 notes

Budget used: 8 searches, 8 extracts (extracts were short passages; several cards rest on search snippets and are marked).

## Topline
- Every major method (Kodály, Orff, Gordon, Suzuki, Dalcroze) agrees on the order: experience sound through singing, speech and movement first; notation comes later and names what the child already knows. Sound-before-symbol is the consensus, not one school's idea.
- Kodály sequence is the most game-ready ladder: sing so-mi, then add la, do, re (pentatonic), and only later fa and ti. The reason given is that half steps are hard for young singers to hit in tune.
- Orff gives the creative ladder: imitate, explore, improvise, compose. Improvisation is taught by question-and-answer inside constraints set by the teacher (for example "fill 8 beats").
- Rhythm: steady beat comes first (kindergarten/age 5-6 spends most of a year on it), then rhythm over the beat. Syllable systems differ (Kodály ta/ti-ti ties a name to each note value; Gordon du/de ties syllables to the beat position). Both are in wide use; pick one and be consistent.
- Solfège: movable do is the Kodály/Gordon norm and trains relative pitch; fixed do is the norm in Romance-language countries (do = C). Hand signs (Curwen) add a height-in-space cue. Our games need a stated choice, probably a toggle.
- Child voices: songs should sit about D4-A4 for under-5s; middle C or lower pushes the voice. Pitch matching develops mostly between ages 4 and 6, and is helped by movement and by wordless syllables.
- Common misconception: young children say "high" for loud (and some languages use the same word). Teach pitch and dynamics side by side, and show pitch with vertical position.
- Harmony enters late and through the ear first: drone/bordun (I and V), then I-IV-V root playing on barred instruments, with chord tones added on top. Roots first, full chords later.

## Cards

### Q1 Methods

#### Comparative review of five early-childhood methods
- URL: https://www.ijrp.org/paper-detail/6668
- Source: IJRP, 2026, review (abstract only read; journal quality unverified)
- Subq: Q1
- Claims:
  - [fact] Kodály stresses literacy and inner hearing via folk songs, hand signs and movable-do solfège.
  - [fact] Suzuki stresses early start, listening, repetition and parent involvement.
  - [fact] Dalcroze joins movement to music to build rhythm and expression; Gordon centres on audiation and sequenced learning.
  - [opinion] The review argues for blending methods rather than choosing one.
- For our games: a good game can mix: Kodály pitch ladder, Orff creative tasks, Dalcroze movement, Gordon audiation (hear it in your head before you play).
- Caveats: abstract only; shallow; no age-specific detail.

#### Kodály concept (practitioner association)
- URL: https://www.oake.org/the-kodaly-concept
- Source: Organization of American Kodály Educators, undated, method association
- Subq: Q1, Q3
- Claims:
  - [practice] Kodály recommended the pentatone (do re mi sol la) for early training because it dominates children's folk music.
  - [practice] Curriculum must be carefully sequenced; success in each step feeds motivation.
  - [practice] Material is folk songs, children's game songs and composed pedagogical exercises; the voice is the main instrument.
- Quote: "Success breeds success – and fosters a love of music."
- For our games: the principle of a fixed, small-step unlock order (few notes, then add one) fits game levels directly.
- Caveats: advocacy source; says the sequence is experience-based, not tied to ages.

#### Orff Schulwerk process
- URL: https://www.classicsforkids.com/orff-approach (also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orff_Schulwerk, https://www.musicedconsultants.net/uploads/1/0/1/6/101642100/dps_elem_music_workshop.pdf)
- Source: Classics for Kids; teacher workshop PDF; Wikipedia. Practitioner/secondary. Search snippets only.
- Subq: Q1, Q5
- Claims:
  - [practice] Four stages: imitation, exploration, improvisation, composition.
  - [practice] Starts with rhythm via speech patterns, then body percussion, then pitched barred instruments.
  - [practice] Improvisation is scaffolded: the teacher sets limits (for example 8 beats) and students answer a given phrase.
  - [practice] Music is built from short ostinati, folk melodies and elemental material, all within singing range.
- For our games: "echo my pattern, then answer it, then make your own" is a ready loop for a tap/piano game, with the game setting the limits.
- Caveats: not read in full; Orff is a process, not a fixed curriculum.

### Q2 Sound before symbol

#### Sound before sight in elementary general music
- URL: https://www.victoriaboler.com/blog/sound-before-sight-in-elementary-general-music
- Source: Victoria Boler, teacher blog, undated, expert practitioner
- Subq: Q2, Q3
- Claims:
  - [practice] Orff, Kodály, Music Learning Theory, Suzuki and Feierabend all follow sound before symbol.
  - [practice] A new pitch (for example la) is first used in a singing game, improvised with, identified by ear, and only then notated and named.
  - [opinion] Learning to read music is like learning to read a language: meaning is built through use first.
- For our games: new note or rhythm unlocks in four steps (play it, play with it, hear it, see it written) before any score is shown.
- Caveats: blog; no age data.

#### Sound before symbol: lessons from history
- URL: https://pianodao.com/2015/10/17/sound-before-symbol-lessons-from-history
- Source: Pianodao, 2015, blog (piano teacher)
- Subq: Q2
- Claims:
  - [fact] Bartók played by ear at 4 and had formal lessons later; Brahms learned informally to 7, formal piano at 7, theory at 10 (as reported in the post).
  - [opinion] Teaching notation before children have observed the sounds it represents is a basic mistake.
- For our games: anecdotes only; use as motivation, not evidence.
- Caveats: historical anecdotes, survivor bias, secondary retelling.

#### Readiness: separating pitch from loudness (pre-reading with high/low lines)
- URL: https://www.janinesmusicroom.com/high-doesnt-mean-loud-clearing-up-the-confusion
- Source: Janine's Music Room, teacher blog, undated, expert practitioner
- Subq: Q2, Q7
- Claims:
  - [practice] Children often use "high/low" to mean loud/quiet; some languages use one word for both.
  - [practice] Fix by teaching pitch and dynamics separately first, then combining them.
  - [practice] A two-line "staff" with two pitches, read left to right, was picked up by most students in one lesson; four beats make a simple frame.
  - [practice] Composition steps: place high/low sounds on four beats, add extra notes for ti-ti, then make some loud.
- For our games: two-pitch, two-line pre-staff is a good first notation step; show loudness by size or brightness, never by height.
- Caveats: one teacher's classroom, age not stated (likely 5-7).

### Q3 Solfège

#### Kodály pentatonic sequence and why
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kod%C3%A1ly_method (also https://www.teaching-children-music.com/2011/01/kodaly-method)
- Source: Wikipedia; Teaching Children Music blog. Secondary. Search snippets only.
- Subq: Q3, Q5
- Claims:
  - [fact] Early Kodály books used the full diatonic scale, but children struggled with half steps and wide range, so pentatonic became the stepping stone.
  - [practice] Order: mi-so (minor third), then la, do, re; fa and ti only after the pentatonic is secure.
  - [practice] Kodály said each nation should build its sequence from its own folk music.
  - [practice] So-mi is the first interval children use naturally in playground calls (as claimed by the teacher video).
- For our games: start melody games with 2-3 notes (so, mi, la), add notes one at a time; delay semitone steps.
- Caveats: Wikipedia plus blog; the intervals-by-ease claim is common but I did not see a controlled study.

#### Movable vs fixed do
- URL: https://www.teaching-children-music.com/2012/10/movable-do-vs-fixed-do
- Source: Teaching Children Music, 2012, blog (practitioner)
- Subq: Q3
- Claims:
  - [fact] Movable do: do is the tonic of any key (relative pitch); Kodály method uses it. Fixed do: do is always C.
  - [fact] Fixed do came from France assigning syllables to absolute pitches; some countries have only solfège names, so fixed do is the local norm there.
  - [opinion] Fixed do suits absolute pitch and dictation; movable do suits singing and sight-singing.
  - [opinion] Fixed do on the piano gives a gentle start in C (so = G, mi = E, la = A).
- For our games: Spanish/European users will expect fixed do; offer a "note names" setting (do=C vs scale degree) and say which is on.
- Caveats: author's opinion; the Spanish context is not covered beyond the general point. See Gaps.

#### Solfège hand signs guide
- URL: https://www.solfegebooks.com/blogs/solfege-hand-signs-guide
- Source: Solfege Books (vendor blog), undated, vendor
- Subq: Q3
- Claims:
  - [fact] Curwen signs (adapted by Kodály) were developed in the 1840s from Sarah Glover's system (confirmed by a second search snippet).
  - [practice] Signs sit higher as pitch goes higher; a correct shape at the wrong height gives incomplete information.
  - [practice] Signs can be used with fixed do, then they name pitches instead of scale functions.
- For our games: height-in-space is a strong, free cue; camera hand-sign recognition is an idea but needs a feasibility check (not this topic).
- Caveats: vendor; Wikipedia and Bubble Box snippets agree.

### Q4 Rhythm

#### Rhythm syllable systems compared
- URL: https://makemomentsmatter.org/classroom-ideas/rhythm-syllable-systems-what-to-use-and-why
- Source: Make Moments Matter, undated, teacher blog
- Subq: Q4
- Claims:
  - [fact] Kodály: quarter = ta, eighths = ti-ti, sixteenths = tika-tika; a syllable names a note value wherever it falls.
  - [fact] Gordon (adapted 1993 from earlier systems, with Froseth and Blaser): beat = du, split = du-de, further = du-ta-de-ta; syllables follow position in the beat.
  - [opinion] Kodály is common in US elementary rooms but critics say it does not scale up; Gordon is said to suit instrumental playing and keep the pulse.
  - [practice] Takadimi is a third option closely related to Kodály.
- For our games: pick one set and keep it. Kodály ta/ti-ti is the most widespread for 4-8 and is easy to show as pictures; Gordon helps beat feel and counting later.
- Caveats: blog; Gordon origin date and claims are the author's.

#### How to teach rhythm in elementary (steady beat first)
- URL: https://beccasmusicroom.com/teach-rhythm
- Source: Becca's Music Room, undated, teacher blog and vendor of lesson packs
- Subq: Q4, Q5
- Claims:
  - [practice] Kindergarten (about 5) and most of first grade (about 6) are spent practising steady beat daily.
  - [practice] Rhythm is introduced through the rhythm of words, then named as ta and ti-ti; manipulatives (cards, popsicle sticks) let children arrange rhythms on beats.
- For our games: beat-keeping must have its own game before rhythm patterns; word chants and physical objects work (links to Brick Beats and Beat Tiles).
- Caveats: vendor of paid packs; US grade-to-age mapping is approximate.

### Q5 Progression

#### Musical Development Matters (UK early years)
- URL: https://early-education.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Musical-Development-Matters-ONLINE.pdf
- Source: Early Education (UK) with music educators, 2021, framework
- Subq: Q5, Q2
- Claims:
  - [fact] Around 40-60 months children match pitch with their voice, sing the contour of known songs and whole songs, and "sing inside their heads".
  - [fact] They move to the pulse, keep their own steady beat on an instrument and tap the syllables of words.
  - [fact] They think abstractly about music (describe it as stories, fast to slow, spiky/smooth).
  - [practice] Use wordless syllables ("ba") for pitch matching; use call-and-response songs; repeat songs.
  - [fact] The framework says bands overlap and are not a checklist.
- For our games: ages are a guide only; adaptive difficulty beats fixed age gates. Use contour and story language for the youngest.
- Caveats: covers under-6s only. No 6-10 ladder found yet.

#### Voice range and pitch matching in under-5s
- URL: https://choralnet.org/archives/578765
- Source: ChoralNet (Lynn Swanson), undated, expert practitioner article
- Subq: Q5, Q3
- Claims:
  - [number] Songs for the youngest should sit near D major; start with D4-A4, then add B4-C5.
  - [fact] Under-5s usually cannot sing middle C without pressed phonation; before puberty the recommended range is roughly B-flat below middle C up to E/F.
  - [practice] Movement and Curwen signs help pitch accuracy; pre-skills include awareness of singing sensations.
- For our games: if a game asks for singing/humming, keep the target range D4-A4 for the youngest and avoid low targets.
- Caveats: secondary summary of research; I did not check the cited studies.

#### Independent accuracy at 4 and 5 (vendor)
- URL: https://themusicclass.com/blog/music-development-early-childhood-four-and-five-year-olds
- Source: The Music Class, undated, vendor
- Subq: Q5
- Claims:
  - [practice] Ages 4-5 are when many children reach "independent music accuracy": singing familiar songs in tune and matching the beat.
  - [practice] Rhythmic accuracy ranges from occasionally to consistently matching a heard beat.
- For our games: tolerate beat error at 4-5; score tolerance should be wide and widen further for the youngest.
- Caveats: vendor, search snippet only.

### Q6 Harmony and chords

#### Functional harmony in the Orff classroom (conference handout)
- URL: https://musiciselementary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/SAMS-IMEA-19-Functional-Harmony.pdf
- Source: conference session handout, 2019, expert practitioner
- Subq: Q6
- Claims:
  - [practice] Order: sing and pat an I-V ostinato (solfa or numerals), transfer to barred instruments, then build I-IV-V by moving through chord changes with the body first.
  - [practice] First play only chord roots on the downbeat while the class sings; goal is feeling when the chord changes.
  - [practice] Then add chord tones in parts (roots in bass, two chord tones above) in key of C with I, IV, V/V7.
  - [practice] A bordun is a tonic (1 and 5) drone; a part that changes chords is not a bordun (from a second workshop PDF).
- For our games: teach harmony as "when does the home sound change", with root-only accompaniment first; triads layered after.
- Caveats: handout fragment; aimed at upper elementary, exact grade not read.

## Gaps
- Q3: nothing on Spanish/European fixed-do practice in schools beyond a general blog claim; no source on how Spanish-speaking children learn pitch names (do-re-mi vs letters).
- Q5: no ladder for ages 6-10 (inner hearing, intervals, reading, improvisation/composition by age); only under-6 data and Kodály grade outlines (an ATU Kodály sequence PDF and a UNT thesis showed up in snippets, unread).
- Q6: little on scales/keys for kids, major vs minor, or accompaniment patterns for a piano player.
- Q7: only one source on misconceptions; nothing on colour or metaphor evidence, or on the "beat vs rhythm" confusion, notation misconceptions (note value vs duration, rests), or stories for theory.
- Q1: Suzuki, Dalcroze and Gordon described only in the abstract; Yamaha/early-childhood programmes not covered. Suzuki's parent-practice routines are not covered.
- No controlled studies on whether sound-before-symbol outperforms early reading; sources are practitioner consensus.
- Contradictions: movable vs fixed do (disagreement is real and context-dependent); Kodály vs Gordon rhythm syllables (each side claims better transfer; no data).
